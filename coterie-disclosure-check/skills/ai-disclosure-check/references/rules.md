## The law in brief

- The duty starts on 10 December 2026. It was added to Australian Privacy Principle 1 by the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 as APP 1.7 to 1.9. [The Act, APP 1.7–1.9]
- It applies to APP entities: organisations covered by the Privacy Act. Most businesses with turnover of $3 million or less are not covered, but some are whatever their size, such as health service providers and businesses that trade in personal information. [OAIC: small business]
- Three conditions must all be met: you have arranged for a computer program to make a decision, or do something substantially and directly related to making it; the decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect someone's rights or interests; and personal information about that person is used in the program. [The Act, APP 1.7–1.9]
- Making a decision includes refusing or failing to make one, and the effect on the person can be good or bad. [The Act, APP 1.7–1.9]
- A decision can be in scope even when a person reviews the output. The OAIC says machine learning or generative AI outputs used in significant decisions would generally be in scope unless subject to extensive human oversight and control. [OAIC fact sheet]
- The duty is about transparency: it sets what your privacy policy must contain. It does not stop you using the software. [The Act, APP 1.7–1.9]
- The OAIC published a fact sheet, a flowchart and updated APP 1 guidelines on the duty on 30 September 2026. [OAIC, 30 Sep 2026]

## The coverage check: questions

Ask these in order, one at a time, offering the options as written. Each answer's
meaning is what to tell the person about that answer.

### Question `turnover`: Is your business's annual turnover more than $3 million?

Help: For the Privacy Act, turnover means all income from all sources. It does not include assets held, capital gains or proceeds of capital sales.

Sources: [OAIC: small business]

| Option id | Option | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| `over` | More than $3 million | Businesses with turnover over $3 million are covered by the Privacy Act, so they are APP entities. |
| `under` | $3 million or less | Most businesses at or under $3 million are exempt, unless one of the exceptions in the next question applies. |
| `unsure` | Not sure | Whether you are covered depends on turnover, so this needs checking. |

### Question `covered`: Does any of these describe your business?

Help: These businesses are covered by the Privacy Act whatever their turnover.

- A health service provider. This includes allied health, complementary therapists, gyms, weight-loss clinics, child care centres and private schools.
- A business that buys or sells personal information
- A contractor providing services under a Commonwealth contract
- A credit reporting body, or an operator of a residential tenancy database
- Accredited under the Consumer Data Right
- Related to a business the Privacy Act covers, prescribed by regulation, or opted in
- A reporting entity under the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006

Sources: [OAIC: small business] [OAIC: who is covered]

| Option id | Option | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| `yes` | Yes, at least one of the first six | These businesses are covered by the Privacy Act regardless of turnover. |
| `amlctf` | Only the AML/CTF one | For a small business, the Privacy Act covers the activities that relate to its AML/CTF obligations, not everything it does. Whether a given decision falls inside that needs advice. |
| `none` | None of these | With none of the exceptions, the business is likely not covered by the Privacy Act. |
| `unsure` | Not sure | Whether one of the exceptions applies needs checking. |

### Question `program`: Do you use software to make decisions about people, or to recommend, score or rank them for a decision?

Help: The OAIC reads “computer program” broadly: rule-based processes, AI and machine learning, ordinary software and apps (a spreadsheet formula counts), and generative AI such as chatbots. Bought software counts as much as software you built.

Sources: [OAIC fact sheet]

| Option id | Option | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| `yes` | Yes | A computer program is involved in making a decision, or in doing something related to it. |
| `no` | No | If no computer program makes or informs decisions about people, the duty is not triggered. |
| `unsure` | Not sure | It is worth listing your tools first: phone agents, booking and quoting tools, CRM scoring, and any spreadsheet that ranks people. |

### Question `personal`: Does that software use personal information about the person the decision is about?

Help: For example their name, address, age, history with you, location, health details or payment record.

Sources: [The Act, APP 1.7–1.9]

| Option id | Option | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| `yes` | Yes | The program uses personal information about the individual, which is the third condition in APP 1.7. |
| `no` | No | If the program does not use personal information about the individual, APP 1.7 is not triggered. |
| `unsure` | Not sure | Check what data each tool reads. Your vendor should be able to tell you. |

### Question `effect`: Could the decision significantly affect the person's rights or interests?

Help: The OAIC says the effect must be more than trivial, with the potential to considerably change the person's circumstances. The Act's examples include decisions that affect someone's rights under a contract or their access to a significant service or support. The effect can be good or bad.

Sources: [OAIC fact sheet] [The Act, APP 1.7–1.9] [OAIC: employee records]

| Option id | Option | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| `yes` | Yes, for example price or contract terms, eligibility, credit, insurance, housing, hiring, or who gets health or disability services first | Decisions like these are on the OAIC's list of decisions generally in scope. |
| `staff` | Only decisions about my own current or former staff, such as pay or performance | A private employer's handling of employee records about current or former staff is exempt from the APPs in some circumstances. Job applicants are not covered by that exemption. This needs advice. |
| `no` | No, the effect on the person is trivial | If the decision could not reasonably be expected to significantly affect the person, APP 1.7 is not triggered. |
| `unsure` | Not sure | The OAIC notes that small effects can add up (its example is a small price difference repeated over a year) and that vulnerable people may be affected more. |

### Question `human`: How is the software's output used?

Help: A person making the final call does not by itself take a decision out of scope.

Sources: [OAIC fact sheet]

| Option id | Option | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| `solely` | The software makes the decision on its own | The privacy policy must list the kinds of decisions made solely by computer programs. |
| `key` | A person decides, but the software's recommendation, score or ranking is a key factor | The OAIC treats a program that advises on or influences the outcome as substantially and directly related to the decision, even with human review. |
| `oversight` | A person decides with extensive oversight: they test the output, weigh other evidence and record why they agree or differ | The OAIC says steps like these could indicate the output was not substantially related to the decision. It is a judgement call, so check it. |
| `unsure` | Not sure | How much weight people give the output decides whether it is in scope. It is worth finding out. |

## What the privacy policy must cover (APP 1.8)

1. The kinds of personal information used in the operation of those computer programs. [The Act, APP 1.7–1.9]
2. The kinds of decisions made solely by the operation of those programs. [The Act, APP 1.7–1.9]
3. The kinds of decisions where a program does something substantially and directly related to making the decision, such as recommending, scoring or ranking. [The Act, APP 1.7–1.9]

## How the OAIC says to write it

- You can group decisions and kinds of information into categories, but the detail must be meaningful to a reasonable person. [OAIC fact sheet]
- Make it clear where more sensitive information, such as health information or biometric templates, is used. [OAIC fact sheet]
- Skip granular technical detail. Tailor it to your business, and let people ask for more. [OAIC fact sheet]
- Trade secrets and commercially sensitive detail can stay out. The fact that you use personal information this way cannot. [OAIC fact sheet]
- If you bought the software, ask the vendor for a plain description of how it can be used to make decisions. The duty generally stays with you, not the vendor. [OAIC fact sheet]
- It applies to decisions made after 10 December 2026, even where the software was set up, or the information collected, before then. [The Act, APP 1.7–1.9]

## The decision list

One row per program or tool. Columns:

| Program or tool | Decision it makes or informs | Personal information it uses | Decides alone, or a person decides? | Could it significantly affect the person? |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Where to look:

- Phone agents and chatbots that answer or sort customers.
- Booking, quoting and pricing tools.
- Scoring or ranking in your CRM or practice software.
- Screening tools for applications, credit, eligibility or hiring.
- Any spreadsheet that ranks or scores people. A formula counts as a computer program.

## Draft wording template

Fill the brackets from the person's own facts. Keep a bracket where a fact is unknown.

```text
Automated decisions
Some decisions about you are made, or partly made, with the help of a computer program. The program uses this information about you: [the kinds of personal information, for example: your contact details, your history with us, your payment record, your location].
Decisions the program makes on its own:
- [one line per kind of decision, for example: the price or payment terms you are offered.]
Decisions a person makes with the program's help:
- [one line per kind of decision, for example: whether we can accept your application, where a person reviews the program's score or recommendation before deciding.]
You can ask us how any of these decisions was made, and you can ask us to correct your information at any time.
```

- This is a draft for your adviser to review, not legal advice. Your adviser settles the final words.
- The last sentence is not one of APP 1.8's three items. The OAIC does say to let people ask for more, so keep it if you can honour it.
- Delete a heading if nothing belongs under it, and keep the two lists separate: the Act treats them as different items.
